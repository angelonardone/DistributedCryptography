A **hardware key** (also called a *hardware wallet*) is a small device that keeps the words of your wallet and its
spending keys **inside itself**. It signs your payments inside itself too. The computer sends it the payment and gets
back the signature, never the keys.

Distributed Cryptography works with three hardware keys:

| | |
|---|---|
| [**Ruby**](/wallet/ruby/) | Our own firmware, based on Blockstream's Jade, on a cheap M5Stack board (about USD 40). It can do everything the application can do. |
| [**Trezor**](/hardware-keys/trezor/) | The Trezor Model T, Safe 3 and Safe 5, with their official firmware. |
| [**Jade**](/hardware-keys/jade/) | The Blockstream Jade, with its official firmware. |

:::caution[Trezor and Jade: tested with the emulators]
Trezor and Jade wallets are in the application since version 1.010. They were tested with the official emulators of
both devices, not yet with physical devices. Try them with a small amount first.
:::

## Why a hardware key matters

A computer runs many programs, reads email and opens web pages. If malware gets in, it can read what is on the
disk and in memory, and it can change what you see on the screen.

**A software wallet** keeps its words in the wallet file, encrypted with your password. To pay, the wallet uses the
keys on the computer. Malware that waits for you to open the wallet, or that records your password, can take them,
and with them your bitcoin.

**A hardware key** changes three things:

- **The keys never reach the computer.** The words are made, written down and typed (to restore) **on the device**.
  Malware on the computer has nothing to steal that can spend.
- **You check the payment on the device.** The device shows the address, the amount and the fee on its own screen,
  and nothing is signed until you confirm there. Malware can change what the computer shows, not what the device
  shows. **Always check the device's screen.**
- **A PIN that can only be tried a few times.** A stolen device asks for its PIN, and after a few wrong PINs it
  erases its keys. Your words bring the wallet back.

So a hardware key is the best place for the bitcoin you want to keep safe. A software wallet is still fine for small
amounts.

## What stays on the computer: the utility keys

Distributed Cryptography does much more than pay: it logs you in to the server without a user name or password,
encrypts your chats, files and notes, and works with your Smart Groups. Asking the device for each of those would
mean a confirmation every few seconds.

So, when a hardware key wallet is opened, the device gives the application a few **utility keys** for those features.
They come from the device's own words, so they are always the same, and **they cannot spend**. They live on the
computer while the wallet is open, as in any wallet. If malware took them, it could read your messages and files,
**but not move your bitcoin**: every payment still needs the device.

Each device gives the utility keys in its own way (see [How each one works](#how-each-one-works)). That is why the
same words in a software wallet give the same bitcoin addresses, but not the same chats, files or server user.

## How each one works

### Ruby

[Ruby](/wallet/ruby/) is our own firmware. We added to it everything the application needs, so a Ruby wallet can
do everything a software wallet can, including every Smart Group.

- **Connection:** USB, as a serial port.
- **PIN:** Ruby protects its words with the PIN plus a secret kept by a *blind oracle*. In a Ruby wallet that oracle
  lives **inside your wallet file**: no server is involved, and Ruby alone, without the wallet file and its password,
  is useless.
- **Utility keys:** Ruby gives them when you type the PIN, with a command we added to the firmware.
- **Smart Groups:** Ruby signs every group type. It registers a group the first time it pays from it, so it recognises
  the group's change. When a group payment offers extra fee levels, Ruby asks once for all of them.
- **Backup:** Ruby can split its own seed for a [Consensus Backup](/groups/consensus-backup/#with-a-ruby-wallet), and
  a backed-up wallet can be restored onto a new Ruby.

:::caution[Ruby is still for testing]
Today's Ruby firmware is a development build, without secure boot or flash encryption. Use it to learn and test, on
TestNet or with small amounts. See [Ruby hardware key](/wallet/ruby/).
:::

### Trezor

A [Trezor wallet](/hardware-keys/trezor/) uses a Trezor as it comes from the factory, with its official firmware.

- **Connection:** USB, through **Trezor Suite**, the official Trezor application, which must be open. Trezor Suite and
  Distributed Cryptography can use the Trezor at the same time.
- **PIN:** the Trezor checks it **by itself**, on its own screen. No oracle and no internet.
- **Utility keys:** the Trezor computes a secret from its words for Distributed Cryptography, without showing
  anything. The utility keys come from that secret, every time the wallet is opened.
- **Smart Groups:** the official firmware signs Taproot payments only the simple way (one key). So a Trezor can be a
  member of a **Legacy Multi Signature Wallet**, but cannot sign for the Taproot group wallets. It never gives its
  seed, so it cannot be backed up in a group: its backup is its words.

### Jade

A [Jade wallet](/hardware-keys/jade/) uses a Blockstream Jade as it comes from the factory, with its official
firmware. Ruby is a fork of this firmware, so they talk the same way, but the official Jade has none of the
additions we made to Ruby.

- **Connection:** USB, as a serial port. Close Blockstream Green while you use it: only one program can use the port.
- **PIN:** the Jade checks it with a *blind oracle*, and you choose which one when you create the wallet:
  - **Blockstream's oracle**, the one the Jade comes with. It needs internet at every unlock, and the Jade keeps
    working with Blockstream Green and other wallets.
  - **The oracle of your wallet**, inside the wallet file, as Ruby does. No internet, but the Jade must be reset first,
    and then only that wallet file can unlock it.
- **Utility keys:** when the wallet is created, the Jade exports a set of 24 words made from its own words (BIP85),
  for this purpose only. It asks you to confirm that **once**. The application keeps them in the wallet file,
  encrypted with the wallet password.
- **Smart Groups:** the same as Trezor. It can be a member of a **Legacy Multi Signature Wallet** (it registers the
  group the first time), but cannot sign for the Taproot group wallets, and it cannot be backed up in a group.

## Comparison

### The device

| | Ruby | Trezor | Jade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firmware | ours, open source, based on Jade | the official Trezor firmware | the official Blockstream firmware |
| Devices | M5Stack Basic Core v2.7 (about USD 40) | Model T, Safe 3, Safe 5. Model One: unlock it in Trezor Suite first. Safe 7: not yet | Blockstream Jade |
| Connection | USB | USB, with Trezor Suite open | USB, with Blockstream Green closed |
| Where the PIN is checked | Ruby + an oracle inside your wallet file | on the Trezor | Jade + Blockstream's oracle (internet) or an oracle inside your wallet file |
| Internet to open the wallet | no | no | only with Blockstream's oracle |
| Wallets of the application on one device | one | several (one per wallet type) | several with Blockstream's oracle; one with the wallet's oracle |
| Utility keys | given by Ruby at every unlock | computed by the Trezor at every unlock, nothing shown | 24 words exported once, at creation (confirmed on the Jade) |
| Your backup | the words alone (no passphrase) | the words alone (no passphrase) | the words alone (no passphrase) |
| Ready for | testing: development firmware | since version 1.010, tested with the emulator | since version 1.010, tested with the emulator |

### Wallet types

| Wallet type | Ruby | Trezor | Jade |
|---|---|---|---|
| BIP86 (Taproot, addresses `bc1p…`) | yes | yes (the default) | yes (the default) |
| BIP84 (native SegWit, `bc1q…`) | — | yes | yes |
| BIP49 (SegWit in P2SH, `3…`) | — | yes | yes |
| BIP44 (Legacy, `1…`) | — | yes | yes |

The same Trezor or Jade can have several wallets side by side, one per type: they share the words, not the
addresses.

The online features, the Smart Groups and the off-line tools need a BIP86 wallet, except for Trezor and Jade: a
Trezor or Jade wallet of **any** type can log in, have contacts, chat, be in a group and use the off-line tools. A
software wallet of another type only receives and pays.

A member who keeps somebody else's backup, or who is in a restore group, is **asked nothing on the device**: those
shares are opened with the utility keys. The wallet that is recovered is the owner's wallet: a software wallet, or a
Ruby. The member's own device does not hold it.

When a Trezor or Jade wallet tries to create or join a group that its device cannot sign, the application stops at
once and says why. Nothing is half done.

### Which one to choose

- **You already have a Trezor or a Jade:** use it. Your payments and your Legacy Multi Signature Wallets are signed on
  it, and everything else in the application works.
- **You want every Smart Group on a hardware key** (the Taproot group wallets, a backup of the hardware key wallet
  itself, the bounty of a Time-Encrypted Vault): only Ruby does that today.
- **You want the cheapest device:** Ruby, on a USD 40 board. Until its signed firmware is out, use it for learning and
  small amounts.

Whatever you choose, **your words are your backup**. Write them on paper, keep them where only you can reach them,
and never type them into a computer.
